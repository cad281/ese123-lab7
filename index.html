Assembly Language & the Buzzer

Your board gets its brain. You plug in the ATmega4809 microcontroller, solder the piezo buzzer, and write your first program in assembly language, the language of the chip itself. The first version toggles the buzzer pin so fast you can’t hear it. Adding delay loops slows it to about 440 Hz, a note you can hear. Then you prepare the surface-mount LEDs for next week.

Plays a quiet square wave at the frequency above. Program 1 is far above human hearing (20 kHz), so it is silent.

  

  

    
Why Program 1 is silent
The loop is only 5 instructions: ldi, out, ldi, out (1 cycle each) and rjmp (2 cycles) = 6 cycles per wave. At 3.33 MHz that is about 556 kHz, far above the 20 kHz limit of hearing. The scope on BUZ shows it; your ears can’t.

    
Why the delay loop works
Each pass through bent: spends 12 nops + dec + brne. Starting r17 at 255 makes 255 passes, about 3824 cycles. Doing that once with the pin high and once with it low makes one wave of 7656 cycles: about 435 Hz, close to concert A (440 Hz).

    
DEC sets Z, BRNE reads it
DEC subtracts 1 and sets the zero flag only when the result is 0. BRNE (“branch if not equal”) jumps back while Z is clear and falls through once Z is set. (The pre-lab table words this backwards; the text below it is right.)

    
20 MHz or 3.33 MHz?
The pre-lab says 20 MHz, but the program never changes the clock settings, and the ATmega4809 starts up dividing 20 MHz by 6. Your measured frequency tells you which: 7656 cycles × 435 Hz ≈ 3.33 million cycles per second. That’s the first report question.

2 · From assembly to machine code, and choosing bits

  
The chip only understands 16-bit numbers. LDI r24,165 is assembled into 1110 1010 1000 0101: 4 bits say “LDI”, 8 bits hold the constant (split in two), and 4 bits pick the register. Only 4 bits for the register is why LDI works with r16–r31 only. Pick a register and a constant:

Bit 0 is the rightmost (least significant), bit 7 the leftmost. There is no “out immediate”, so you always load a register with LDI first, then OUT it to the port.

4 · Board assembly: buzzer, R15, the microcontroller and the LEDs

  

  

  

    

      
    

    

      
Surface-mount LED orientation

      
The two pads are not identical: the cathode (−) pad has little “horns”. The LED’s green (or black) mark is the cathode. Green mark → horned pad. On the solder side, with the board oriented as in the handout, the six horizontal LEDs point their green marks left, the two vertical ones up.

      
Mixed colours look alike when unlit. DMM Shift → Cont (diode test) lights the LED so you can see its colour; swap probes if it doesn’t light (it only conducts one way).

      
Six LEDs count seconds, one shows USB power, one shows the alarm is armed. Some colours don’t show through some acrylic covers, so check the demo board at the back of the lab first.
